## His Perspective on Key Themes

### EFB and Cockpit Software
- Values integration that doesn't add cognitive load or muddy the primary purpose of
  the app. An integrated feature is only valuable if it doesn't degrade the core workflow.
- Believes the EFB market is shifting from feature-count competition to data quality
  competition — incumbents should take this seriously, especially for operators who feel
  the gap acutely (e.g. business aviation, high-frequency operators).
- Standalone apps can deliver insight; integrated tools change workflow — and workflow
  is where habits actually form. Integration into the broader EFB ecosystem is the
  architectural step that produces durable adoption.
- The Garmin walled ecosystem is a real but segment-specific constraint, not a
  platform-level dealbreaker for challengers.

### Airline-Built Software (e.g. Cathay Technologies EFF)
- Airline-built tools entering the commercial market carry genuine credibility from
  real operational use — but it's a double-edged sword: buyers may worry the solution
  is tailored to the original airline's specific operations, or that the originating
  airline will always be prioritized as the main customer.
- The internal-use heritage matters in initial credibility but has to be backed by
  adaptation depth in new markets.

### Fuel Efficiency and Pilot-Facing Analytics
- Crew engagement with fuel-efficiency tools must be designed in from the start — not
  treated as a change-management problem after contract signing. The data pipeline is
  the easier engineering problem; what determines whether the tool moves behavior is
  designing feedback a captain on their fourth turnaround will actually read and act on.
- Long-haul carriers likely benefit more from pilot-facing coaching tools than LCCs —
  longer sectors mean more opportunity to act on fuel-saving techniques, and the
  per-flight savings potential is larger.
- Aggregate fuel reports produce aggregate behavior; individualized feedback produces
  different engagement entirely.

### AI in Aviation (Cockpit and Ops)
- The industry remains heavily skeptical of AI-native startups — only 4% of aviation
  professionals say AI startups build the best flight ops software. This skepticism is
  real and valid, not just conservatism.
- AI adoption barriers are primarily liability and accountability (41%), followed by
  data quality and integration (23%), then trust and culture (20%), and regulatory
  timelines (16%).
- AI safety impact is currently perceived as strongest in maintenance and anomaly
  detection (52%) — well ahead of cockpit decision support (14%) or preflight
  planning (17%). The industry sees AI as a maintenance-first problem.
- This gap between where AI safety interest is focused (maintenance) vs. where the
  cockpit needs it (decision support) is a meaningful contrast worth exploring.
- AI in the cockpit should prove itself on lower-stakes applications (document search,
  briefing support) before moving toward decision-critical in-flight territory. That
  sequencing is realistic, not a criticism.
- On the question of where AI should go first in the cockpit: 40% say keep AI out
  entirely, 40% say maintenance and documents, 20% say pilot decision support — a
  split that reflects genuine industry uncertainty.

### ATC / ATM and Infrastructure
- FF-ICE and ATM modernization are structural developments, not just regulatory
  compliance exercises — the flight planning stack that sits on top of them will need
  to adapt.
- Space-based ATC communication is a real infrastructure shift, not just incremental
  improvement.

### Advanced Air Mobility / eVTOL
- The biggest barrier to commercial eVTOL launch is traffic management infrastructure
  (37%), ahead of aircraft certification (27%), public trust (20%), and vertiport
  access (17%).
- Economic viability at scale is the biggest hurdle for commercial electric regional
  aviation (45%) — well ahead of certification (27%), charging infrastructure (20%),
  and ops/dispatch software (7%).

### Flight Ops Data and Analytics
- Fuel efficiency and savings is where flight data analytics delivers the most value
  today (59%), followed by safety and risk monitoring (24%), maintenance and
  reliability (11%), and pilot performance feedback (5%).
- The biggest operational data blind spot is ground and ramp operations (53%),
  followed by aircraft parts and MRO (31%), crew and staff movement (12%), and
  terminal/passenger flow (4%).

### Vendor Landscape
- Dedicated industry vendors are still seen as building the best flight ops software
  (46%), but airlines building in-house are a close second (38%) — a significant shift.
  Big tech cloud providers are at 13%, AI startups at just 4%.
- Non-traditional data vendors (like Meteomatics) are finding more traction as
  operators look for a more complete operational picture. API-accessible, high-quality
  data from outside the traditional avionics stack is increasingly viable.
- From experience on the vendor/delivery side: integration is rarely the hardest
  part technically — getting data presented at the right moment in the right workflow
  context without adding cognitive load is the real challenge.

### Procurement and Adoption
- Crew engagement is consistently underestimated at the procurement stage.
- From bid and delivery work: the hardest integration is rarely the data feed itself —
  it's contextual presentation in the right workflow moment.
- From time on both product and delivery sides of flight ops software: airline
  software risk management is conservative by design — validated, familiar systems
  are preferred even when newer alternatives are technically superior.

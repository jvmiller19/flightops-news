The *Financial Times* reported on October 16 that Lufthansa has engaged investment banks to explore a potential sale of the Lido and NetLine software businesses within its Lufthansa Systems subsidiary. Lufthansa is exploring a sale worth close to €1 billion, with Lido handling route and fuel planning for each flight and NetLine managing scheduling, crew assignment, and operations recovery — and private equity investors are seen as the likely buyers. The Sirax revenue accounting product could also be included in a deal. Discussions remain at an early stage with no final decision taken, though the businesses reportedly generate well above €200 million in revenue and roughly €50 million in EBITDA — a profile that private equity buyers tend to find attractive.

For anyone who has spent time inside the flight operations software world, the scale of what's on the table here is worth sitting with for a moment. Lido Flight 4D is used by more than 125 airline operators flying more than 7,700 aircraft, and carries over 2,700 configurable parameters and 50 integration interfaces. That's not a niche product — it's deep infrastructure for a significant slice of global commercial aviation. The Lido ecosystem includes not just the core flight planning engine but the Lido mPilot EFB, which delivers the planned route and operational documents directly to crew tablets in the cockpit, making the platform one of the few in the market that genuinely spans from pre-flight optimization all the way to the flightdeck.

## Why the Ownership Question Actually Matters for the Product

The deal framing — Lufthansa shedding non-core assets to focus on its airline operations and fund the TAP acquisition — is straightforward enough. The objective is to let the company focus on its core passenger and cargo business, and Lufthansa also needs capital to acquire a significant stake in TAP, the Portuguese flag carrier. But what gets less attention in the deal coverage is what a change of ownership would mean for the product roadmap.

Flight planning software earns trust slowly and loses it fast. Airlines don't switch flight planning systems on a whim — the certification dependencies, aeronautical database integrations, crew familiarization requirements, and dispatch workflow entanglements make these platforms among the stickiest in aviation IT. That stickiness is actually what makes Lido attractive to a PE buyer. But it's also exactly what creates risk when ownership changes: the product investment thesis that made sense inside a company with deep aeronautical roots doesn't automatically survive a financial buyer looking at EBITDA multiples and margin expansion.

Having spent years on the delivery and product side of flight operations software, including time working directly on Lido Navigation at Lufthansa Systems, I've seen how much of what makes these platforms genuinely work lives in the institutional knowledge of the teams that build and support them — the edge cases, the airline-specific workarounds, the hard-won understanding of how a particular carrier's ops team actually uses the tool under pressure. That knowledge doesn't show up in the balance sheet, and it tends to get restructured away in the cost-savings phase of PE ownership. That said, I don't think operators should be overly alarmed here. The customer base is deep, the contractual relationships are long-cycle, and a buyer sophisticated enough to pay close to €1 billion for this asset understands that the revenue depends on not breaking what works. My read is that the roadmap pressure won't be severe — the stickiness that makes Lido attractive to a buyer is the same stickiness that gives the product team some insulation.

The same logic applies to Lido mPilot. There's a reasonable argument that a PE owner might de-emphasize the EFB layer in favor of protecting margins in the higher-revenue core planning platform, but I think that misreads the product's value proposition. The integration between mPilot and the flight planning engine is precisely what differentiates Lido from platforms that treat the cockpit layer as a bolt-on. A buyer that understands what they're acquiring should keep that integration intact as a competitive priority, not treat it as overhead.

## The Competitive Ripple

The timing creates an interesting competitive dynamic. Jeppesen ForeFlight launched its first AI agents for commercial aviation on October 6, 2026, and the Thoma Bravo deal that brought it under PE ownership was completed in November 2025 at a $10.55 billion valuation — a deal that already shifted the competitive landscape for flight planning and EFB platforms. Now Lido, one of the two or three platforms that can credibly compete at the large-carrier level, is potentially heading toward its own ownership transition.

For airlines currently in contract renewal cycles, or evaluating platforms for new fleet additions, this kind of uncertainty in a key vendor's ownership trajectory is exactly the kind of thing that slows procurement decisions. My honest advice to an airline mid-evaluation between Lido and a competing platform would be to proceed normally. A deal at this stage, however it resolves, isn't going to cause operational disruption to existing customers or dramatically change what Lido is capable of delivering in the near term. Whether the broader uncertainty benefits NAVBLUE or simply extends evaluation timelines at airlines that were already on the fence is harder to predict — but it's a variable that wasn't in the market last month.

The story is still early, and Lufthansa may ultimately decide not to proceed. But the fact that they've hired banks suggests this is a serious exploration, not a casual consideration. For the 125+ operators whose flight planning and flightdeck workflows run on Lido, the next few months are worth watching closely.

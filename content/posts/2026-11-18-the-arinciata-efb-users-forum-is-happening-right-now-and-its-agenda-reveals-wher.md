The fall EFB Users Forum — the ARINC IA AEEC event held in joint activity with IATA — opened yesterday in Dallas and runs through Thursday. It draws airlines, airframe manufacturers, regulators, and technology suppliers into the same room specifically to work through the unglamorous but consequential parts of EFB technology: standards, interoperability, connectivity, and the operational problems that vendors rarely advertise on their product pages.

I think the agenda is worth reading closely, because it's one of the most reliable signals we have for where the EFB market's real competitive pressure is building.

## The Agenda Tells a Different Story Than the Product Brochures

<cite index="30-1">This year's forum has put connectivity solutions, human factors and workload considerations, emerging technologies like GNSS interference detection and real-time turbulence reporting, and operator experiences at the center of its agenda. <cite index="30-6">The EFB Expo element specifically features cybersecurity, reducing fuel burn, satcom evolution, and data sharing.

That cluster of topics isn't accidental. It reflects where operators are actually feeling friction. GNSS interference has moved from an edge case to a routine operational concern across European and Middle Eastern airspace, and the question of how EFB platforms detect, surface, and respond to that interference is genuinely unsolved in a standardized way. Real-time turbulence reporting is similarly upstream of the product — the data exists, the platforms exist, but the interoperability between turbulence data feeds and EFB software remains uneven across operators. Satcom evolution, with bandwidth expanding rapidly via LEO constellations, is fundamentally changing what cockpit-side data delivery is capable of.

The standardization forum is where these problems get turned into addressable specifications rather than vendor-by-vendor workarounds. <cite index="32-2,32-3">The EFB Subcommittee that met Sunday in advance of the main forum develops ARINC Standards that enable standardized EFB installation, connectivity, and interoperability, covering both hardware and software specifications including data protocols and application control.

## Data Sharing Is the Competition Now

<cite index="30-4">The forum enables aircraft operators to voice their preferences regarding EFB connectivity, ground interfaces, application development, and data sharing between applications — and that last phrase is where I'd focus attention. The data-sharing problem between EFB applications is structural, not incidental. When a pilot switches between chart display, NOTAM review, weather overlay, and performance tools, those applications typically have no shared data context. Each represents its own data silo, its own update cadence, and its own decision about what to surface and when.

My view is that the EFB market has been shifting for a while from feature-count competition to data quality and data-sharing competition. The vendors who understand that the cockpit workflow is a continuous information environment — not a collection of discrete apps — are the ones building architecture that will hold up over the next decade. The vendors still competing on individual feature lists are optimizing for procurement checklists rather than actual cockpit utility.

<cite index="29-7,29-8">At this year's forum, Lufthansa Systems is presenting Lido mPilot, demonstrating integrated charting and navigation capabilities including terminal charts, dynamic enroute maps, an interactive airport moving map, operational documents, and weather information — all within a single pilot workflow. That framing of integration within a single workflow rather than a feature list is exactly the direction the market is pushing.

## Why the Forum Format Still Matters

<cite index="30-2,30-3">The forum's core function is industry coordination — facilitating collaboration between airlines, manufacturers, suppliers, and regulators to shape the evolution of EFB hardware and software, with a specific focus on solving common problems through standardized approaches via the EFB Subcommittee.

That function is easy to underestimate. Having spent time on both the vendor delivery side and the product side of flight ops software, I've seen how much of the integration friction operators deal with traces back to the absence of a shared standard — not to any individual vendor's unwillingness to build good software. When the forum produces a new ARINC specification, it doesn't make headlines. But it changes what the next generation of EFB deployments can actually do without custom engineering on every side of every integration.

<cite index="30-5">The forum's operational problem-solving objective explicitly includes reducing CO2 footprint and improving pilot efficiency — which puts fuel-efficiency data and cockpit-side sustainability tools squarely on the agenda alongside the more infrastructure-oriented connectivity work. That combination is the right framing. The cockpit data problem and the fuel efficiency problem are the same problem, looked at from different angles.

The forum runs through Thursday. I'll be watching for any standardization outputs or operator-side statements about EFB connectivity priorities that emerge from the working sessions.

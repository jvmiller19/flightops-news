When FlyExclusive announced in March 2026 that it would make its Contrails flight management system available to other Part 135 charter operators at no cost, the natural reaction was skepticism. <cite index="28-9">"Adding AI to a 20-year-old software business model doesn't change the business model," CEO Jim Segrave said at the time — a pointed jab at incumbents. The Fly Alliance FMS adoption, announced at NBAA-BACE on October 21, was the first real-market validation of that strategy. And now the November 13 management services agreement between the two companies — which builds directly on that software relationship — makes the strategic logic considerably clearer.

## The Actual Business Model

<cite index="28-1">Contrails FMS shares real-time aircraft availability across participating operators to reduce empty legs and fill unmet charter demand. <cite index="28-10">It's being offered without software licensing fees, per-seat charges, or feature gates. That's not philanthropy — it's a network play. <cite index="29-7,29-8">According to FlyExclusive, operators manage their own fleets and schedules independently, often leaving aircraft underutilized and repositioning opportunities unrealized. Contrails FMS lets participating operators and brokers access authorized, real-time aircraft availability across participating fleets, making it easier to book flights on one another's aircraft. The free FMS is the entry point; the shared availability network is the value layer that benefits FlyExclusive most directly as the platform's originating operator.

<cite index="31-4">Contrails was developed by combining FlyExclusive's operational experience with intellectual property acquired from Volato Group, including its Mission Control flight management system. <cite index="33-6">Rather than recalculating schedules from scratch when conditions change, the system precomputes valid options across the full fleet, allowing near-instant schedule recovery during irregular operations. That architecture is non-trivial — but it's worth noting that the underlying IP came from an acquisition rather than purely internal development.

## What This Means for the Competitive Landscape

From spending years on the product and delivery side of flight ops software, I'd say the most interesting thing here isn't the technology — it's the go-to-market architecture. Traditional business aviation flight ops vendors charge recurring licensing fees and compete on feature differentiation. FlyExclusive is effectively subsidizing software adoption with its own operational network revenues, which changes the competitive equation entirely for smaller Part 135 operators who might otherwise find enterprise-grade scheduling and disruption recovery software hard to justify.

<cite index="36-4,36-5">Fly Alliance operates more than 100,000 flight hours and 50,000 dispatched legs across charter, aircraft management, jet cards, fractional ownership, and aircraft sales — and its commitment covers a full operational transition rather than a limited trial. That's a meaningful proof point. When an operator that size replaces its entire FMS rather than running a pilot, it signals that Contrails is production-ready and that the network value proposition is compelling enough to justify the switching cost.

<cite index="20-6">The November management services agreement builds on that earlier software adoption, expanding the relationship beyond aviation technology to encompass comprehensive flight operations and business infrastructure. In other words, the FMS was the opening move; shared operations management is where FlyExclusive is actually heading with this relationship.

## The Bigger Strategic Question

The poll data I track on who builds the best flight ops software is relevant here: 46% of aviation professionals still trust dedicated industry vendors most, but 38% now trust airlines or operators building in-house — a gap that continues to close. FlyExclusive's Contrails strategy is a live experiment in what happens when an operator-built platform is priced to undercut incumbents entirely.

The risk, as I see it, is adoption at scale. Getting a second operator onto your FMS is a commercial win. Getting fifty operators to share real-time availability data across a competitive marketplace requires a level of trust that takes time to build — and the value of the network only compounds once critical mass is reached. FlyExclusive is essentially betting that its operational credibility and zero-cost model will attract enough Part 135 operators fast enough to make the availability-sharing layer genuinely useful before a well-funded incumbent responds with a comparable network offering of its own. That's a reasonable bet, but it's a bet on network effects, not just software quality — and network effects in business aviation are notoriously slow to materialize.

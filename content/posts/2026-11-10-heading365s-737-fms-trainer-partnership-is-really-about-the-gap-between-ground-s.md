There's a gap in how most airlines train pilots on the FMS, and it's not the gap they tend to talk about. The conversation usually focuses on simulator hours — how many, how well distributed through a type rating program, whether the full-flight simulator faithfully represents the aircraft's specific software version. What gets less attention is the procedural fluency problem that happens *before* a pilot ever climbs into the sim: the need to have FMS keystrokes, page structures, and data entry sequences close enough to automatic that the simulator session can actually be used for judgment and decision-making, rather than hunting through menus.

Austrian startup Heading365 is building around exactly that gap. <cite index="44-2">The company announced a partnership with Spain-headquartered Aviation Exchange Group to provide its Boeing 737 FMS Trainer PRO to the training organisation's airline customers. <cite index="44-3">Announced during the European Aviation Training Summit (EATS 2026) in Portugal, the agreement will allow Aviation Exchange Group to offer customised FMS training software supporting pilot preparation for Boeing 737NG and 737 MAX operations.

## What the Software Actually Does — and Why the Configurability Matters

The mechanics here are worth unpacking, because the value proposition isn't just "FMS practice on a laptop." <cite index="44-5">The software is intended to complement existing ground school, type rating, and recurrent training programs by enabling pilots to practise FMS procedures independently before attending full-flight simulator sessions. That sequencing is the real point. A pilot who arrives at the sim already comfortable with the basic data entry workflow — departure setup, lateral revisions, performance page entries — can use simulator time for the genuinely hard problems: abnormal procedures, decision-making under pressure, CRM. The sim hour is expensive; the cognitive load of learning button sequences in it is a waste of that resource.

<cite index="44-4">Under the agreement, Aviation Exchange Group will make the software available to its customers, with Heading365 providing configurations adapted to each airline's aircraft systems, operating procedures, and training requirements. That last clause matters more than it might look. Airlines operating 737NGs and MAXes don't all use identical nav databases, and many have airline-specific procedures or custom FMS configurations that differ meaningfully from the generic Boeing baseline. <cite index="44-6">The platform supports airline-specific navigation databases and aircraft configurations, allowing operators to align the training environment with their operational requirements. That's a real differentiator — a training tool that teaches generic Boeing FMS procedure is useful, but one that mirrors what a pilot will actually see on their own airline's aircraft is considerably more so.

## The Deeper Operational Question

From time spent on the product and delivery side of flight operations software, the configurability argument is one I find genuinely credible — but it's also where the execution risk lives. Building and maintaining airline-specific database configurations across multiple operators isn't a one-time engineering lift; it requires ongoing update cycles, coordination with airlines' own nav data suppliers, and version control discipline that small vendors sometimes underestimate. That's not a reason to dismiss the approach, but it's the question I'd want answered before recommending it at scale.

The European training software ecosystem has been slowly professionalizing around exactly this kind of tool — purpose-built, procedurally specific, complementary to full-flight simulation rather than competing with it. What Heading365 is doing fits a coherent model of how pilot proficiency actually develops: distributed practice on procedure, concentrated simulation on judgment. The Aviation Exchange Group distribution channel gives them reach into airline training organizations across Spain and presumably further into Europe, which is the right geography for a startup trying to build density before moving to larger markets.

The FMS is, in many ways, the most consequential piece of software in the cockpit — it's where the flight plan lives, where route revisions happen, where performance data gets entered and acted on. Getting pilots genuinely fluent on it before the simulator clock starts running isn't a nice-to-have; it's a training efficiency question with real operational stakes. That Heading365 is building around that insight, and that the announcement came out of a European training summit rather than a Silicon Valley pitch deck, is part of why the story is worth following.

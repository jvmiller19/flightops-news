There's a meaningful difference between an airline buying fuel efficiency software and an airline group standardizing on a single fuel efficiency platform across all of its carriers. When IAG announced on September 29 that it had selected OpenAirlines' SkyBreathe for deployment across Aer Lingus, British Airways, Iberia, LEVEL, and Vueling, that distinction matters more than the headline number.

## A Portfolio Deal, Not a Point-Solution Purchase

IAG has selected OpenAirlines' SkyBreathe fuel-efficiency platform for use across its five airlines, covering more than 600 aircraft. That scale alone is notable, but the more commercially significant detail is the architecture behind the deployment. The rollout introduces a common data and reporting framework across the group, allowing IAG's airlines to measure fuel-efficiency performance more consistently and share initiatives. That's a different procurement logic than buying a tool to reduce fuel burn at one carrier — it's treating the software layer as group-wide operational infrastructure, the same way a large airline group might standardize on a single crew management or flight planning system.

SkyBreathe is already in use at Aer Lingus, Iberia, LEVEL, and Vueling, with British Airways expected to complete its rollout in the coming months. That sequencing suggests the deal formalized a consolidation of deployments that were already happening organically across the group — which is actually a stronger commercial story than a clean greenfield win, because it implies OpenAirlines earned expansion across the portfolio one airline at a time before landing the group-level commitment.

## What the Cockpit-Facing Layer Actually Does

SkyBreathe analyses data including flight plans, weather conditions, and aircraft performance to identify potential fuel-saving opportunities while maintaining safety standards. The analytical engine sits in the background, but SkyBreathe's pilot-facing module — MyFuelCoach — is where the flight deck angle comes in. MyFuelCoach is an application pilots use during briefing and debriefing phases to see their individual performance, with precise recommendations based on their journey to help them make more informed decisions and improve environmental performance. That closes the loop between fleet-level data analysis and the individual crew member who can actually act on a recommendation in the cockpit. Without that layer, you have a reporting tool; with it, you have something that can actually change behavior at the point of flight.

Having spent time on the product and delivery side of flight ops software, I'd note that the hardest part of a deployment at this scale isn't the data integration — it's getting crews to engage with the feedback loop consistently enough that the platform generates real savings rather than just reports. Individual performance feedback during briefing and debriefing can be genuinely effective at changing behavior, though, and I think that's an underappreciated part of what makes the MyFuelCoach approach worth taking seriously. SkyBreathe is used by more than 80 airlines worldwide, and the platform harnesses advanced algorithms, artificial intelligence, and big data analytics to identify fuel-saving opportunities, delivering actionable recommendations to both operational teams and flight crews. That installed base gives OpenAirlines something genuinely valuable to offer a group like IAG: benchmarking data from a global operator community that no single-airline deployment can replicate.

## What This Means Competitively

OpenAirlines has had a notable run in 2026 — Loganair, Pegasus, and now IAG in the same year. From the outside, that reads less like organic market pull and more like a deliberate expansion push, and I think the distinction actually matters when you're evaluating the platform as a competitor. A company growing that quickly across very different carrier profiles — a Scottish regional, a large Turkish LCC, and one of Europe's biggest groups — is likely making intentional moves on market positioning, not just responding to inbound interest. That kind of momentum tends to be self-reinforcing: each reference customer makes the next conversation easier to open.

For IAG, the shared data and reporting framework is an advantage on both sides of the equation. From a flight planning and cockpit-data perspective, group-wide standardization gives pilots and operational teams consistent performance benchmarks across carriers, which is genuinely useful when you're trying to understand whether a fuel-saving initiative is working or just reflecting differences in route mix and fleet type. There's also an obvious commercial logic driving the decision — a common framework simplifies procurement, vendor management, and cross-carrier performance reporting in ways that matter to a group CFO.

The platform is being deployed at Aer Lingus, British Airways, Iberia, LEVEL, and Vueling as airlines face continued pressure from volatile fuel prices. That volatility context matters commercially: when fuel costs are unpredictable, the business case for a platform that promises measurable reduction becomes easier to close across a finance committee.

For the broader market, the interesting question is whether this represents the beginning of a consolidation pattern among European airline groups. Lufthansa Group, Air France-KLM, and others face the same multi-carrier, multi-AOC complexity that makes group-standardization appealing. Whether OpenAirlines can replicate the IAG playbook — organic adoption at the subsidiary level, followed by a group-level framework deal — is probably the most important strategic question for the company heading into 2027.
